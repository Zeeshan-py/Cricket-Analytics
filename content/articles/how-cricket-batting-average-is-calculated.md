Batting average is one of cricket's simplest and most widely used measures. It tries to answer one basic question: how many runs does a batter score per dismissal?

The formula is:

| Metric | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Batting average | Runs scored / Times dismissed |

If a batter scores 400 runs and is dismissed 10 times, the batting average is 40.00. If the same batter scores 400 runs but is dismissed only 8 times, the average is 50.00.

## Why not outs matter

Cricket average does not divide runs by innings. It divides runs by dismissals. This is why not-out innings are important.

Imagine a batter has these scores:

- 40
- 20 not out
- 60
- 10 not out

The batter has scored 130 runs in four innings, but has been dismissed only twice. The average is 65.00, not 32.50.

## What batting average tells you

Batting average is useful because it rewards both scoring and survival. A player who scores heavily while protecting their wicket will usually have a strong average.

It is especially useful in longer formats, where occupying the crease has high value. You can explore batting leaderboards on the [batting analytics page](/analytics/batting).

## What batting average does not tell you

Average does not explain scoring speed. A player averaging 45 from slow innings may have a different role from a player averaging 35 at a much higher tempo.

Average also needs context:

- Match format
- Batting position
- Venue and conditions
- Era and scoring environment
- Team role

For faster formats, strike rate often needs to be read alongside average.

## Summary

Batting average is runs divided by dismissals. It is powerful, easy to understand, and still useful, but it should not be used alone. Pair it with strike rate, match role, and format context when comparing players.
